Our Retirement Hacienda

Two flanks of the same Colombian valley, twelve real parcels, and one honest trade-off to settle between us. Here's where the evidence actually points — and what it would cost to build the thing we keep talking about.
We're leaning warm — Moniquirá. The numbers say that's defensible. It wins on health care and costs the same. It loses on one thing: drought.

The choice, in one paragraph

Both flanks get the same rainfall — 1,860 mm on the warm side, 1,826 mm up in Arcabuco. So this was never really about which place is wetter. Moniquirá sits 900 m lower and is 4.8 °C warmer, so more of its rain evaporates. In a normal year that costs nothing at all — Moniquirá has zero dry months and ends the year in surplus. In an El Niño drought year it goes to ten difficult months where Arcabuco has three. Against that, Moniquirá has a proper hospital in town — first and second level, 118 services — and Arcabuco has a village clinic and a 45-minute drive. That's the whole decision: better health care now, versus better water in the bad years.

Four parcels worth arguing about

Out of twelve we looked at properly — every photo, every listing, every agency. Six have sat unsold with one agency for a year, so nobody should be paying asking price. All twelve, with the photo-by-photo reads, are in the dossier.

Best land to build on

H · the geotech lote

Moniquirá · 8 ha · COP 720 M
~19 °C, warm9,000 COP/m²15 min to town

Open, gently-sloping ridgetop pasture with mature shade trees and a long view over the valley. Already fully fenced, 15 paddocks, an aljibe and a well. The photographs show clean, clear ground — no rock outcrops, no scrub, nothing to clear before you start.

Why it's here: It comes with a geotechnical study of the soil and of the water, already done and handed over. That is the single most valuable document in the whole search — every other parcel would have to pay for it, and it is the study that tells you whether you can actually build. The listing also says outright that the price is negotiable.

What it costs: Land 720 M + roughly 2,070 M to build the community (below) ≈ 2,790 M all-in.

Before you fall in love: Read the existing soil and water studies first — they are why you would pay the premium over Papayal. Then the last 2 km of unpaved access, and the title.

Cheapest usable land

G · Papayal

Moniquirá · 9.1 ha · COP 390 M
~19 °C, warm4,281 COP/m²cheapest per m²

A broad flat valley-bottom pasture with big shade trees and grazing cattle, and a real stream running over cobbles in deep shade — the water in the photos is genuinely there.

Why it's here: Best price per usable hectare anywhere in the search, and it has been sitting unsold with the same agency for twelve months.

What it costs: Land 390 M + about 2,100 M to build ≈ 2,490 M all-in — including a 45 M karst contingency the other parcels do not need.

Before you fall in love: The photos show limestone karst — a deep rock ravine, an exposed outcrop with a cave recess. That means possible voids under foundations, groundwater that moves fast and unpredictably, and septic fields that can reach the aquifer quickly. Get a geotechnical and hydrogeological survey before offering, not after. And treat “90% usable” as a seller's number: a real share of it is gorge and steep forest.

The one we could actually divide

N · the panela farm

Moniquirá · 41 ha · COP 1,700 M
~19 °C, warm4,150 COP/m²41 hectares

A working sugar-cane farm on semi-flat ground with good soils, its own wells, a vereda aqueduct, a maintained gravel road — and a complete trapiche: mill, moulding room, furnace. Two simple houses already stand on it.

Why it's here: At the same price per m² as Papayal it is four and a half times the land, and it is the only parcel we found above the 9.38 ha legal subdivision floor. That matters enormously: everywhere else, the group has to buy one parcel and hold it jointly through a company. Here each family could own its own titled plot. It has also been on the market fourteen months.

What it costs: Land 1,700 M + about 2,250 M ≈ 3,950 M all-in — but you are buying an income and existing buildings alongside the land.

Before you fall in love: Whether the cane operation is genuinely running or just standing. Soil quality claims. And get a surveyor to confirm the 41 ha before assuming the subdivision maths works.

The two things the evidence actually turns on

Short version here. The full workings — sources, methods, every number — are in the report.

Water, and specifically drought. Rain versus evaporation, month by month; red means the ground is drawing down more than it receives. Villa de Leyva — the pretty one — is in deficit most of the year and rationed drinking water in 2016. Both our flanks sit in surplus, which is why they're the shortlist. The gap only opens in a bad year, and that's the whole argument between them.

Terrain — and this one surprised us. We pulled real 30 m satellite elevation and measured the slope where parcels are actually sold, rather than across the whole mountain. The two flanks are nearly identical — typical ground is about 10° on both. What differs is the bad end: Arcabuco has two and a half times more genuinely steep land. So it isn't "flat versus steep". It's that in the highland, picking the right parcel matters much more, because you're likelier to be shown something difficult.

One warning that applies to every warm-flank parcel: the water is abundant but not drinkable as it arrives. Of 23 rural supplies in Moniquirá only three have a working treatment plant, and all four the health authority monitors are rated high risk with no treatment at all — including the one behind parcel G's "vereda water point". Solvable, and costed below: about COP 11–15.5 M for a plant, plus 600,000 a year. Budget it, don't assume it.

Now the fun part — what would we actually build?

One casa comunal in the middle — the big kitchen, the long table, the fire, a couple of guest rooms for whoever's visiting, the laundry and the workshop. Then three private houses scattered around it at 90–140 m, far enough that you can't hear each other, close enough to walk over for dinner without thinking about it. Everything fed by gravity from one tank uphill, so the water works when the power doesn't.

The layout isn't decoration — it's built around the constraints we actually found. The tank sits uphill of everything so the whole system is gravity-fed. The septic fields are pushed well away from the stream. Nothing is inside the 30 m ronda hídrica, the legal no-build strip along any watercourse. The forest edge stays where it is, for shade and for the birds.

Three households sharing. After phase 2 you are living on the land — the communal house and the first private house are finished — for roughly COP 680 M each (≈ EUR 158,000 at the 2026 average rate). The last two houses can wait for whenever. All-in, finished, on parcel H: about COP 2,790 M — near enough COP 930 M (≈ EUR 216,000) per household. Running costs land around COP 26 M a year for everything including a part-time caretaker, which is under 9 M each.

These are 2026 published Colombian rates, not quotes — rural building swings hugely with access and ground conditions. Treat them as a budget envelope for the conversation, ±30%, and get real quotes before anyone transfers anything.

What happens next

  1. Settle the climate question between us — warm Moniquirá or cool Arcabuco. Everything else follows from that, and the scorecard above is the tool for that argument.
  2. Ask the agencies for coordinates and the matrícula inmobiliaria on the two or three we like. We can't check boundaries, protected areas or real slope without them — and one listing already has the wrong vereda on it.
  3. Read H's soil and water studies. They already exist. That is a free look at the single question that decides whether a parcel is buildable.
  4. Get a water test and a Corpoboyacá concession check on whichever parcel leads.
  5. Then, and only then, make an offer — well under asking, on anything that's been sitting for a year.
